Who Are Your Neighbors

A map of people who write like you.
You get to see who is near you. Nobody, including you, gets told why.

The descriptions are readable in full. They were written by a language model from public posts, and they can be wrong about a person.

Nearestscore

 

 

Topic is what a person keeps coming back to. Worldview is what they think and how they think things connect. Style is temperament, and the kinds of argument they are drawn to.

 

Score is standardised, not a percentage: 0 is how close an average pair of people are, and 1.0 means one standard deviation closer than average. Negative means further apart than average. A rank is only ever a rank — there is no explanation attached to it, and there will not be one.

How you would get on this map

You cannot yet. Nothing described in this section is built. There is no way to contribute today, and the steps below are what the process is intended to be, not what it does.

  1. Ask X for your archive. It is a setting in your account. They take about a day, then email you a link to download it.
  2. Drop the tweets.js file from it onto the page. The file itself will never be uploaded. It will be opened and read inside your own browser, on your own machine.
  3. Your browser will do the cutting down. Reposts of other people's writing will go. So will posts under five words, and posts that are nothing but a link. Where you replied to yourself over and over, the chain will be stitched back into one piece of writing. From what is left, about 400 pieces will be picked, spread across the years. Only that sample and your handle will leave your machine.
  4. A fixed model will read the sample and write five short descriptions of you. Those descriptions, and the numbers made from them, will be kept. The sampled posts will be deleted at that point. They are not stored, not archived, and not held for later.
  5. Your profile will go on the map, drawn as contributed. That mark records one thing: that you brought your own archive to the page, rather than having it taken from a public one. It does not record that anybody checked whose archive it was.

There is no sign-in, ever. The site will not ask you for a password, and there is nothing to log into, so it never holds a credential of yours that could later leak. The other half of that is worth saying out loud: nothing checks whose archive it was. No identity check is performed at any point, and contributing is not treated as proof of anything.

Your posts are deleted, and that is not reversible. Keeping only the descriptions and the numbers is the point of the design, but it has a real cost: if the instructions given to the model are ever changed, everyone has to contribute again. There is nothing left to quietly re-run, because the inputs are gone. That is the trade, stated plainly.

Some of the profiles on the map above were seeded rather than contributed, and are drawn as hollow circles. Those people uploaded nothing. Their posts were taken from a public archive by whoever built this page, and they did not choose to be here. They are marked differently for that reason and for no other.

The seeded profiles come from the Community Archive, where those people published their whole posting history in the open for anyone to read and analyse. That is what it is there for. Being named on someone else's map of who resembles whom, and described on it in prose you did not write, is a further step, and nobody on this page agreed to it. So if you are on this map and would rather not be, say so and you will be removed. No reason needed and none will be asked for.

How the map is built

You ask X for a copy of your archive. A few days later they send you a file. You drop that file onto this page. Nothing leaves your computer when you do that — the file is opened and read inside your own browser.

Most of the archive is then thrown away. Reposts of other people's writing go, because they are not your words. Posts under five words go. Posts that are nothing but a link go. Where you replied to yourself over and over, that chain is stitched back into a single piece of writing, because that is how it was meant to be read. From what survives, about 400 pieces are picked, spread evenly across the years, so that one loud month cannot speak for you.

Those 400 pieces are handed to a language model — a program that reads text and writes text back. It is the same program every time, given the same instructions every time, so that two people are described on the same terms. It writes five short descriptions of you:

  • what you keep returning to
  • what you actually think
  • how you think the world works — what causes what
  • your temperament
  • which ways of arguing you warm to, and which you cannot stand

Each of those five descriptions is then turned into a long list of numbers. That conversion is called an embedding. The arrangement is built so that two pieces of writing that say similar things come out with similar numbers. That is the entire trick: similar writing, similar numbers. Once you have numbers, closeness is just arithmetic.

Then your posts are deleted. What is kept is the five descriptions and the five lists of numbers. The archive is gone, and it cannot be reconstructed from what is kept. That is a design choice with a cost attached: if the instructions to the model are ever improved, everyone has to contribute again, because there is nothing left to re-read.

People whose numbers land close together are neighbours. You see their names, their rank, and a score, and never a reason for any of it. The descriptions those numbers were made from are published in full on their own page, but nothing on the map is annotated with them, and no rank here arrives with an explanation attached.

The picture at the top of this page is worked out three times over: once for each corner of the triangle. Those three arrangements are settled in advance and then left alone. As you move the dot, each person slides between their three places, in proportion to how much each corner counts at that moment. Nothing is being recalculated while you drag — you are looking at a blend of three fixed pictures. That is why points glide rather than jump, and why dragging back puts everybody exactly where they were.

Where this actually stands

It does not work yet, and it might not. Two questions have to be answered with numbers before any of this is worth showing:

Does the same archive describe the same person twice? The model is run several times over the same posts. If the five descriptions come out meaningfully different each time, then the description is about the run, not about you, and everything downstream is noise. There is a threshold this has to clear, and it has not been measured yet.

Do the five descriptions say five different things? If what you return to, what you think, and how you argue all turn out to be the same measurement wearing three hats, then the triangle at the top of this page is decoration and should be removed rather than dressed up. Also not measured yet.

Until both are answered, there is no map, and the honest thing to do is say so at the top of the page rather than at the bottom.
